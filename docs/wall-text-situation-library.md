# Wall-of-Text Situation Library

## Purpose

Wall-of-Text should make a reader recognise a moment from their own life before
it connects that moment to a product. A useful post is not a feature statement
with a person pasted onto it. It is a specific situation that creates a small
question, tension, choice, or realisation the reader wants resolved.

This library is a planning aid for the 200-idea pool. It is deliberately not a
copy template or a list of claims that may be made about a business.

## Source boundary

The reference corpus at `D:/WOT_all_supplied_images_extraction.md` is a
collection of 468 social-post transcriptions across 17 profiles. It teaches
scene construction, not product facts, voice, claims, or wording to copy. Its
own `[PARTIAL]` labels and quoted posts are source material, not instructions
for the generator.

The strongest recurring mechanism in that corpus is:

```text
recognisable role + concrete object or event + immediate tension + realisation or decision
```

For example, its useful patterns include a creator manually doing an awkward
editing task, a writer discovering their characters have created a plot
problem, and a person opening a familiar app only to encounter an unwanted
memory. Those are scenes; the products and phrases in the source are not ours
to reuse.

The corpus also proves why surface variation is not enough: several posts
repeat the same "designers use X instead of Y" scene with only small wording
changes. A plan must count situations, not synonyms.

## The situation card

Every planned idea should have a private card. The short 8-14 word `contentIdea`
is only the card title; it should never be the writer's whole brief.

| Field | What it answers | Example shape |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Business truth | What approved fact can safely support the post? | One verified capability, pain, audience, or differentiator |
| Reader / role | Whose day is this? | Founder, solo creator, account manager, client approver |
| Workflow stage | Where are they in the work? | Planning, drafting, review, approval, publishing, learning |
| Trigger | What just happened? | A client changes direction, a meeting moves, a post is paused |
| Concrete object | What makes the scene visible? | Calendar, draft, brief, tab, approval note, comment thread |
| Tension or choice | What is difficult now? | Speed versus fit, action versus rework, certainty versus guessing |
| Consequence | What does the reader risk or feel? | Lost time, scattered context, hesitation, a weak decision |
| Reader realisation | What small insight earns the read? | The system is the friction; the next decision can be simpler |
| Allowed product bridge | How may the approved fact connect? | One natural connection only, never a stack of features |

The first eight fields create the reader's scene. The last field keeps the
business connection truthful. Do not use a private card as permission to invent
proof, outcomes, metrics, or a second product capability.

## Twenty situation families for a 200-idea library

Use each family for up to ten genuinely different cards. A variation must
change at least three meaningful elements: role, workflow stage, trigger,
object, tension, consequence, or realisation. Changing only verbs, adjectives,
or the opening does not make a new situation.

| # | Situation family | Reader-recognisable moment | Factual bridge, if supported | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | The blank starting point | Someone opens a plan and cannot tell what deserves attention first | A clearer planning or organisation capability | "Everything in one place" with no moment |
| 2 | The changed afternoon | A meeting, message, or request rearranges work already planned | A truthful way to update or view work | Treating change as a guaranteed productivity win |
| 3 | The account-switch cost | A person changes between brands, clients, or audiences and loses context | A supported organisation or context feature | Repeating "three accounts" with different numbers |
| 4 | The stalled approval | A draft waits because the decision-maker cannot see what matters | A supported review or collaboration fact | Blaming a role or promising instant approval |
| 5 | The last pre-publish pause | Someone rereads a ready draft and doubts whether it fits the audience | A supported quality, review, or context fact | Generic "post with confidence" language |
| 6 | The manual assembly loop | A recurring small task keeps being rebuilt from notes, tabs, or messages | One supported automation or preparation fact | Listing multiple capabilities in one card |
| 7 | The missing handoff | A new person opens work without the reason behind the earlier decision | A supported shared-context or workflow fact | Vague "team alignment" claims |
| 8 | The feedback spiral | A comment or edit creates more questions because the original intention is unclear | A supported review or versioning fact | Turning feedback into a villain |
| 9 | The calendar collision | Two valid priorities compete for the same slot or attention | A supported scheduling or visibility fact | Another generic "busy calendar" scene |
| 10 | The launch pivot | A launch changes, but the surrounding content still points to the old plan | A supported update, review, or planning fact | Claiming a launch will be successful |
| 11 | The founder trade-off | A founder must choose between serving the product and explaining it | A supported reduction in planning effort | "Founders are always too busy" as a stereotype |
| 12 | The client-status question | A client asks what is happening, and the answer is scattered across workspaces | A supported source-of-truth fact | Invented reporting or analytics claims |
| 13 | The channel-fit question | One message is fine in one context but feels wrong in another | A supported audience, brand, or planning fact | Treating every channel as identical |
| 14 | The reusable-draft illusion | Reusing a draft feels efficient until it no longer matches this moment | A supported editing, planning, or context fact | A blanket claim that reuse is bad |
| 15 | The invisible decision | A team keeps revisiting a choice because nobody can see what was decided | A supported organisational fact | Generic "collaboration is hard" copy |
| 16 | The end-of-day residue | Small unfinished decisions accumulate and make tomorrow feel heavier | A supported planning or visibility fact | Forced burnout language |
| 17 | The metrics ambiguity | A person sees a result but cannot tell what to change next | An approved analytics or learning fact, only if present | Unsupported performance promises |
| 18 | The source-material gap | A writer has a task but not enough detail to make it sound like the business | A supported business-context or briefing fact | Inventing the missing detail |
| 19 | The routine that became friction | A normal ritual has quietly grown into unnecessary maintenance | One factual simplification or workflow fact | Selling the product as a personality trait |
| 20 | The confidence recovery | A small, concrete clarity moment lets someone make the next decision | A supported fact that enables that clarity | Overstated transformation or guaranteed outcomes |

These families are not a requirement that every business use every row. A
business with no approved analytics fact should not receive family 17. The
business context determines the valid families; the family only supplies a
human lens.

## How to make ten cards from one family without repetition

Take family 5, the last pre-publish pause. Ten cards should not all be "a
manager pauses a post." Their scene cards might differ in:

| Variation axis | Example of a real difference |
| --- | --- |
| Role | Solo founder, agency account lead, in-house marketer, subject-matter expert |
| Trigger | A customer comment, a client revision, a new audience segment, an upcoming event |
| Object | Caption, post calendar, draft, approval note, product announcement |
| Tension | Accuracy, brand fit, timing, ownership, clarity, risk |
| Consequence | Rework, delay, uncertainty, a weak handoff, a missed moment |
| Realisation | Check the context, not just the grammar; a prepared draft still needs judgment |

Two cards are too close when the reader would describe both with the same
sentence even if their wording differs. They are meaningfully different when a
reader could say, "That is a different moment in my week."

## Writer guidance: scene first, product second

1. Start with the visible moment, action, or decision.
2. Keep one central tension; do not cram several pains into the post.
3. Let the approved product fact support the thought naturally. It can be
   absent when the scene is useful without it.
4. Use ordinary language and a complete thought rather than a slogan.
5. Preserve the scene when paraphrasing the card. Do not replace it with a
   generic capability statement such as "ready-to-post content saves time."

The writer is free to choose wording, rhythm, and the smallest relevant subset
of the card. It is not free to silently turn an approval bottleneck into a
generic automation pitch.

## Flexible quality rubric

Use this review after writing, not as a rigid formula before writing.

| Question | Pass condition | Retry only when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Is there a real scene? | A reader can picture the role, action, or trigger | The copy is only a capability or abstract slogan |
| Does it retain the card? | The same human moment and central tension remain, in natural wording | The scene has been replaced by another scenario |
| Is the claim safe? | It uses only the assigned approved fact | It adds an unsupported feature, proof point, or outcome |
| Is it one thought? | The message has a single understandable point | It stacks unrelated pains or features |
| Is it natural? | It reads like a person noticing something useful | It becomes a forced template, CTA, or marketing mini-story |
| Is it distinct enough? | Its situation signature differs from recent cards | It is the same scene with shallow word substitutions |

Related themes should pass. Exact wording, every private field, a product
mention, and a prescribed sentence shape should never be required. The review
exists to catch obvious drift, not to make Wall-of-Text mechanical.

## Planning coverage for 200 ideas

Use the 200-card plan as a library, not a single chain of synonyms:

```text
business context
  -> approved facts and claims to avoid
  -> eligible situation families
  -> 200 private situation cards
  -> short contentIdea titles
  -> writer
  -> flexible fidelity review
  -> measured visual layout
```

Before activating a plan, inspect a coverage sheet with these counts:

- cards by role and workflow stage;
- cards by situation family and tension;
- repeated role + trigger + tension signatures;
- short idea similarity warnings;
- final-copy fidelity to its assigned situation card; and
- final-copy layout outcomes.

The first four are planning diagnostics, not automatic rejections. Their role
is to reveal concentration early enough for a writer or reviewer to make a
good editorial decision.

## Recommended implementation direction

Use this library to guide human planning and future editorial improvements. It
is not injected wholesale into generation prompts. Any runtime use should
select only business-supported reader categories and relevant scene mechanics,
while keeping exact-copy protection separate from editorial variety.
